# Small Story, Bigger Picture

A storytelling technique for creators whose educational/tip content isn't landing.
Start with a small, seemingly insignificant story, then make it bigger as you speak
until it becomes a lesson that applies to almost everyone watching.

## Why it works

- **Stories carry lessons better than tips.** A tip asks the viewer to accept advice;
  a story lets them feel something. The best content leaves people thinking and
  feeling differently than when they started.
- **Small starts create a bigger emotional journey.** The distance between "a client
  mentioned the hot chocolate" and "service is really about making people feel looked
  after" is what makes it land. A big dramatic opener has nowhere to grow.
- **Realizations feel like revelations, not preaching.** Humans hunt for meaning.
  When the creator presents the lesson as something they *just realized* ("and that's
  when it hit me…"), viewers connect the dots at the same moment and feel like they
  uncovered it themselves.

## The structure

1. **The small story (hook).** A specific, concrete, slightly surprising everyday
   moment. Real detail — the hot chocolate, the cardboard box, the spilt coffee.
   Keep it low-stakes; the smallness is the point.
2. **The turn / realization.** "What surprised me was…" / "And that's when I
   realized…". Name the insight in one plain sentence.
3. **Widen it.** Show that it applies beyond the creator's situation — give 2–3 quick
   examples across different people, roles or industries until it feels universal.
4. **Land it back on the viewer.** One line that makes them look at their own life or
   business differently, then a light CTA (question, save, follow).

## Examples (from the source reel)

- **Service business:** A Google review didn't mention the service — it mentioned the
  hot chocolate she made the client → service isn't just the thing you deliver, it's
  making people feel looked after → how dog groomers, hairdressers, cleaners can do it.
- **Parent creator:** Planned an amazing day out, but the kid wouldn't leave the house
  because they were playing with a cardboard box → we're always trying to make happy
  kids happier.
- **Hairdresser:** A client apologized for her grey roots → women are always
  apologizing for how they look.
- **Anyone:** Spilt coffee on the laptop → a bigger lesson about backing up your work.

## Workflow

1. **Find the small story.** If the user has one, use it. If not, help them mine it:
   ask about small mistakes, surprising client comments, little moments from their
   week, things that annoyed or delighted them. Offer 5–8 story *prompts* specific to
   their niche. Never invent a personal anecdote and present it as true — when you
   write an example story for them, mark it clearly as a template
   (`[FILL IN: your real moment]`) so they swap in something that actually happened;
   the technique only works if the story is real.
2. **Find the bigger picture.** For each small story, propose 1–3 possible bigger
   meanings and pick the one closest to what the creator teaches or sells.
3. **Write it** in the creator's voice: first person, conversational, spoken out
   loud, present the lesson as a realization rather than advice. Avoid "Here's a
   tip" / "You should" framing in the first half.

## Output format

For a short-form video (~30–60 sec, ~90–150 words):

```
### <working title>
**Small story (hook):** "<opening line — concrete, specific, small>"
**The turn:** "<the realization, one sentence>"
**Bigger picture:** <2–3 spoken lines widening it, with quick examples>
**Land it:** "<line that turns it back on the viewer>" + <CTA>
**On-screen text:** <short overlay>
**Caption:** <1–3 sentences>
```

For LinkedIn/text posts, use the same four beats as short paragraphs, opening line
being the small story.

When giving multiple ideas, list them as: small story → bigger picture (one line each),
then write the full script for the one the user picks.
